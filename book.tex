\chapter{Preface}

So, you're a physicist looking to dive into the world of machine learning. You have a deep understanding of calculus, are familiar with experimental data-fitting methods, and have mastered concepts like entropy and non-linearity---yet you have only a vague idea of how a machine learning algorithm is actually written and how it works? Then this book is for you. And the good news is: your learning curve will grow fast. I've been in your shoes. I used to view machine learning as a distant horizon, but I soon realized that the knowledge I'd gained in physics made it incredibly easy to quickly master the concepts and engineering behind the field. So, come along and be amazed.

\chapter{Few-neuron networks}
\label{chap:fundamentals}

Toy-models are useless for real-world applications, but are wonderful for didactic purposes. In this chapter, I use few-neuron models to present {\it the} fundamental concepts for any advanced topic in ML theory.

\section{Single-neuron ML}
\label{sec:single-neuron}

You have been using a single-neuron network since the first time you fitted experimental data, but perhaps no one ever told you that. Let's recall the basics. You have a set of $N$ experimental data points
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:xy-dataset}
    (x_i,y_i)
\end{equation}
through which to fit a linear function
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:linear-activation}
    f(x)=ax+b.
\end{equation}

The Mean Squared Error Loss, defined as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \label{eq:mse-loss}
        L_\text{MSE}(a,b) &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N[y_i - f(x_i)]^2 \\
                        &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N[y_i - (ax_i + b)]^2
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
tells us how distant the curve $f(x)$ is from the datapoints. Fitting $f(x)$ to the datapoints $(x_i,y_i)$ means minimizing $L_\text{MSE}(a,b)$ with respect to $a$ and $b$, that is,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:single-neuron-backpropagation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial(a,b)}L_\text{MSE}(a,b) = 0.
\end{equation}

No surprises so far, right? Well, what if I tell you the above is the simplest possible neural network? Indeed, it is a single-neuron network, where \eqref{eq:linear-activation} is the activation function, \eqref{eq:mse-loss} is the loss function and \eqref{eq:single-neuron-backpropagation} is a very rudimentary backpropagation method.

Solving \eqref{eq:single-neuron-backpropagation} for $a$ and $b$, which, in this simple case is possible analytically, fixes the parameters to $a_0$ and $b_0$
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \label{eq:a0b0}
        a_0 &= \frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2} \\
        b_0 &= \bar y-a_0\bar x
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
and we end up with a model $f_0(x) = a_0x+b_0$ (in \eqref{eq:a0b0}, $\bar x$ and $\bar y$ are the mean values of the $x_i$ and $y_i$ datapoints). This model is a single neuron, no hidden layers: it receives one input parameter and outputs a number. $f_0$ has learned from data \eqref{eq:xy-dataset} through \eqref{eq:single-neuron-backpropagation} and can now predict on any given $x$. Pretty cool, huh? The concepts were sitting there all the time. Take a moment to re-read the definitions and let's jump to the second simplest model: a two-neuron network.


\section{Two-neuron ML}
\label{sec:two-neuron}

Our neuron from Sec. \ref{sec:single-neuron} was of the form $f(x)=ax+b$ (always think of it as receiving one value $x$ and, upon training for $a,b$ parameters, outputting another value $a_0x+b_0$). Let us now place a second neuron, $g$, before it, so that $g$ receives the input and $f$ produces the final output. This new neuron will be of the form $g(x) = \sigma(cx+d)$, where $\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})$. The need for $\sigma$ will be elucidated later; for now, just accept it as a magical ingredient. In fact, it is the so called neuron's activation function!

Placing one neuron before another means composing the two functions, that is, given input $x$, the network outputs $f(g(x))$. In other words, input $x$ feeds a (hidden) layer $g$, which applies its weight $c$ and bias $d$, activates through $\sigma$ and feeds output layer $f$, which in turn applies weight $a$ and bias $b$ and outputs $\hat y$. Layer $f$ has identity operator $\mathds{1}$ as its activation function. A visual diagram may help.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tikzpicture}[node distance=10mm]
        \node (in) {$x$};
        \node[neuron, right=of in] (g)
            {$cx+d$ \nodepart{two} $\sigma$};
        \node[neuron, right=of g] (f)
            {$az+b$ \nodepart{two} $\mathds{1}$};
        \node[right=of f] (out) {$\hat y$};

        \draw[signal] (in) -- (g);
        \draw[signal] (g) -- node[above, font=\footnotesize] {z} (f);
        \draw[signal] (f) -- (out);

        \node[below=2mm of g, font=\footnotesize] {hidden layer $g$};
        \node[below=2mm of f, font=\footnotesize] {output layer $f$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
    % \caption{Each neuron is a box with two compartments: the affine part, which
    % carries the trainable parameters, and the activation function applied to its
    % output. Here $g$ activates with $\sigma$, while $f$ activates with the
    % identity, so that $f$ is nothing but the linear fit of
    % \cref{sec:single-neuron}.}
    \label{fig:two-neuron-network}
\end{figure}

Our task now is to optimize the network for parameters $a,b,c,d$ over training data. The loss function is similar to \eqref{eq:mse-loss}, just generalized for the two-neuron case,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mse-loss4}
    L_\text{MSE}(a,b,c,d) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N[y_i - f(g(x_i))]^2.
\end{equation}

This time, however, MSE minimization, stated as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:two-neuron-backpropagation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial(a,b,c,d)}L_\text{MSE}(a,b,c,d) = 0
\end{equation}
cannot be solved analytically as in \eqref{eq:a0b0}, and numerical methods must be used. The recipe is as follows: start at a certain initial position $(a,b,c,d)$ on the four-dimensional parameter space, then slightly change each coordinate by an amount according to\footnote{Picture this process as finding a minumum in a graph. For reference, in Sec. \ref{sec:single-neuron} the graph of the loss function was a two-dimensional paraboloid with hypersurface $(a,b,L_\text{MSE}(a,b))$ embedded in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$; in this two-neuron scenario, the graph is a four-dimensional hypersurface embedded in $\mathbb{R}^{5}$. The paraboloid has a clear, well-defined minimum; this time, there may be several local minima plus saddle points and plateaus, and which one you land in depends on the starting point.}:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \label{eq:mse4-partials}
        a&\to a-\alpha\frac{\partial L_\text{MSE}}{\partial a}\\
        b&\to b-\alpha\frac{\partial L_\text{MSE}}{\partial b}\\
        c&\to c-\alpha\frac{\partial L_\text{MSE}}{\partial c}\\
        d&\to d-\alpha\frac{\partial L_\text{MSE}}{\partial d}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The term $\alpha$ is called the learning rate (haleluiya, a name you've heard before, now in a clear context!) the derivatives of $L_\text{MSE}$ in \eqref{eq:mse4-partials} points to the max uphill on the hypersurface, so the minus sign will move the initial coordinate to a new position where $L_\text{MSE}$ is lower. After repeating this procedure a couple of times, \eqref{eq:two-neuron-backpropagation} will be solved to a certain desired precision (yes, I am oversimplifying, this step is a billion-dollar industry).

% 2) That's (full-)batch gradient descent, also called steepest descent — "batch" because each step sums over all $N$ points. Alternatives:

% Mini-batch SGD — gradient from a random subset per step. Cheaper, and the noise helps escape saddles. The practical default.
% Momentum / Nesterov — adds inertia; literally restores the term that overdamped descent dropped.
% Adam, RMSProp, Adagrad — per-parameter adaptive learning rates. Adam is today's standard.
% Second-order — Newton, Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, L-BFGS. Worth naming LM for your audience: it's what scipy.optimize.curve_fit runs, so your reader has already used a fancier optimizer than \eqref{eq:mse4-partials} without knowing it.

Let's dive a little into the algebra of this example, as it will allow us to explore one very fundamental concept: backpropagation. The partial derivatives for this case can be taken explicitly:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:chain-partials}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial a} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial a} = -\frac{2}{N}\sum_i\big[y_i-f(g(x_i))\big]g(x_i)\\
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial b} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial b} = -\frac{2}{N}\sum_i\big[y_i-f(g(x_i))\big]\\
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial c} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial g}\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial c} = -\frac{2a}{N}\sum_i\big[y_i-f(g(x_i))\big]\sigma'(cx_i+d)x_i\\
        \frac{\partial L}{\partial d} &= \frac{\partial L}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial g}\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial d} = -\frac{2a}{N}\sum_i\big[y_i-f(g(x_i))\big]\sigma'(cx_i+d)
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

None of these derivatives are useful right now. I do not intend to make predictions on any fake dataset with this toy model. Yet \eqref{eq:chain-partials} are the materialization of a very important concept: backpropagation.
